Family Resource Guide · The OASIS Method™

What to do when a loved one comes home.

A practical guide for families managing a medical transition at home — the logistics nobody hands you in a discharge folder. Free to use, whether or not you ever work with us.

Start Here

If you are reading this at midnight the day before a discharge, read only Section 2. The rest will keep.

Most families arrive at this moment the same way: a fall, a diagnosis, a decline that finally crossed a line. The medical system does its job and then hands the rest to you — the equipment, the schedule, the vendors, the sibling phone calls, the bathroom that suddenly isn't safe.

That handoff is not a care problem. It is an operations problem, and it is solvable. This guide walks the same ground we walk with families, in the same order.

Your family deserves more than a folder of discharge papers and a prayer.

Do first

Safety, bathroom access, and who is in the house overnight. Nothing else matters until these three are answered.

Do this week

Turn medical instructions into a written daily routine. Order equipment. Name one point person.

Do this month

Money, documents, vendor coverage, and a plan for the caregiver who is absorbing all of it.

How to use this guide. Print it. Write on it. Bring it to your next appointment. If any section makes your stomach drop, that is usually the section to start with — and a reasonable thing to bring to a free 30-minute call.

The First 72 Hours

The window where most households either find their footing or start improvising. Work top to bottom.

Before they leave the hospital

  • Get the discharge summary in writing, including the medication list and every follow-up appointment.
  • Ask the case manager directly: what level of help does this person need, and for how many hours a day?
  • Confirm what is ordered — home health, physical therapy, equipment — and who is delivering it, when.
  • Ask what the transfer looks like: can they bear weight, use stairs, get to a toilet unassisted?
  • Ask what would send them back to the hospital, and what number to call before that happens.

The 24 hours before they arrive

  • Clear one path: bed to bathroom. No rugs, no cords, no side tables, lit at night.
  • Decide where they will sleep. If stairs are out, the living room becomes the bedroom — do it now, not at 11 PM.
  • Confirm equipment is in the house and assembled, not on a truck.
  • Stock three days of easy food and any supplies (briefs, wipes, gloves, chux pads) you would rather not run out for.
  • Build the medication schedule on paper, by time of day, not by prescription bottle.

The first night

  • Name who is on duty overnight. Write it down. Rotating "whoever wakes up" is how burnout begins.
  • Put a phone, water, light, and a way to call for help within arm's reach of the bed.
  • Do a dry run of the walk to the bathroom while everyone is awake.
  • Write down what actually went wrong. Day two is when you fix it.
The 22-hour problem. A home health aide may come for two hours a day. The other twenty-two belong to the family. Plan for the twenty-two, not the two.

Questions to Ask Before Discharge

Copy these into your phone. Ask them out loud, and write the answers down.

AskWhy it matters
"What exactly can they do without help — walking, stairs, toileting, dressing, bathing?"This is the real definition of how many hours of coverage you need.
"What has been ordered, and who is responsible for delivering it?"Ordered is not the same as scheduled. Get names and phone numbers.
"Is home health approved, for how many visits, and for how long?"Home health is usually intermittent and time-limited. Know the end date on day one. [verify]
"What is covered, and what will we be paying for ourselves?"Prevents the invoice that arrives three weeks later.
"What does recovery realistically look like at 2 weeks, 6 weeks, 3 months?"Tells you whether you are planning a season or a new normal.
"Who do we call at 9 PM on a Saturday?"The answer should not be "the emergency room."
"Can we get this in writing?"Always. Memory fails in hospital hallways.

Making the Home Work

Most home injuries after a discharge are not medical events. They are furniture, footwear, and lighting.

Room by room

ZoneWhat to change
BathroomGrab bars anchored into studs (never suction cups). Raised toilet seat or commode. Shower chair and handheld sprayer. Non-slip mat inside and outside the tub.
BedroomBed height so feet reach the floor with knees at 90°. Clear path to the door. Lamp reachable from the bed. Nothing stored on the floor.
Hallways & stairsRemove throw rugs entirely. Tape or remove cords. Motion-sensor night lights. Handrail on both sides if stairs are unavoidable.
KitchenMove daily items to waist height. Nothing that requires a step stool. A seat within reach of the counter.
Living areaOne firm chair with arms at the right height — deep sofas trap people. Clear walker turning space.
EntryThreshold ramp if needed. Working exterior light. A safe, known route for EMS to reach them.

The five cheapest changes that matter most

  1. Throw rugs gone. All of them. Today.
  2. Night lighting from bed to toilet.
  3. Non-slip footwear with a back — no socks, no backless slippers.
  4. A grab bar where they actually reach for the towel rack.
  5. A working phone or call button within arm's reach at all times.

Who's Who on the Care Team

Families lose weeks to calling the wrong person. Here is who does what.

Hospital case manager / discharge planner

Sets up what happens at discharge. Your best leverage point — but only while your loved one is still admitted.

Home health agency

Skilled, intermittent, physician-ordered visits — nursing, PT, OT. Usually time-limited. Not a babysitter, not overnight coverage.

Private-duty caregiver / aide

Hourly non-medical help: bathing, meals, transfers, companionship. Typically paid privately or via long-term care insurance.

Physical & occupational therapy

PT rebuilds movement. OT rebuilds daily function — dressing, bathing, kitchen tasks. Ask for OT specifically; families often don't.

Palliative care

Comfort and symptom support alongside active treatment. Not the same as hospice, and not limited to end of life.

Hospice

Comfort-focused care when treatment shifts to quality of life. Includes team support for the family, not only the patient.

Elder law attorney

Powers of attorney, healthcare directives, asset and benefit planning. Engage earlier than feels necessary.

Care navigator (that's us)

The operational layer around all of the above: assessment, home setup, equipment, vendor coordination, routines, family alignment.

Where Gilt fits. We are not a nurse, an agency, or a placement service, and we do not give medical advice. We translate medical instructions into daily systems and make sure the pieces are actually connected. We work alongside your medical team, never in place of them.

Questions worth asking early

  • Is there a long-term care policy anyone has forgotten about? Check old files and premium withdrawals.
  • Did they ever serve in the military? Ask — adult children often don't know the details.
  • What is the monthly number the household can actually sustain for a year?
  • Who has legal authority to sign and to move money if this person can't?

The Document Binder

One binder or one shared folder. Every family member knows where it is.

Medical

Diagnosis list, current medications with doses, allergies, physicians and phone numbers, insurance cards, recent discharge summaries.

Legal

Healthcare power of attorney, financial power of attorney, advance directive / living will, HIPAA authorization, will or trust location.

Financial

Bank and benefit accounts, recurring bills, insurance policies (including any long-term care policy), tax preparer contact.

Household

Vendor list, equipment rentals and return dates, caregiver schedule, home access details, pet and plant instructions.

The 2 AM test. If a stranger had to take over tonight, could they find the medication list, the doctor's number, and who has authority to decide? If not, that is this week's project.

Running the Family Meeting

Most caregiving conflict is not about love. It's about undefined roles.

An agenda that works

  1. Facts first. One person reads the current medical picture out loud. No debate during this part.
  2. What the week actually requires. List the tasks and the hours. Write the real number.
  3. Who covers what. Named person, named day, named task. "Helping when I can" is not a role.
  4. Money. What it costs, who is paying, how decisions get made when it changes.
  5. The out-of-town rule. Distance is not exemption. Money, scheduling, research, and vendor calls travel fine.
  6. Next check-in. Put it on the calendar before anyone leaves.

Ground rules to read aloud

  • The primary caregiver reports what is happening; they do not defend it.
  • No decisions about someone who is able to decide for themselves.
  • Disagree with the plan, bring an alternative with hours and dollars attached.
  • One point person owns communication with the medical team. One.

Caregiver Sustainability

The plan fails when the person holding it up does. Roughly 53 million Americans provide unpaid care to an adult family member each year — most with no professional support.

Signs you are past capacity

  • Sleep is broken most nights, and you've stopped noticing.
  • Your own appointments have been cancelled twice or more.
  • Resentment shows up toward the person you're caring for — then guilt about the resentment.
  • You can't name the last time you were off duty for four uninterrupted hours.
  • Work performance has slipped and you're covering for it.

What actually helps

  • Scheduled relief, not offered relief. A recurring block on the calendar beats "call me if you need anything."
  • Give tasks, not feelings. When someone asks how to help, hand them a specific errand, shift, or phone call.
  • Respite options. Adult day programs, short-term respite stays, and paid coverage for a fixed window.
  • Your own clinician. Around 40% of family caregivers report symptoms of clinical depression. This is a load problem, not a character problem.
  • Offload the logistics. Coordination is the part that can genuinely be handed to someone else. It's the part we take.

When to Escalate

General guidance, not medical advice. When in doubt, call the clinical team — or 911 for anything sudden or severe.

What you noticeWhy it matters
A fall — even without obvious injuryFalls repeat. One fall predicts the next; the plan needs to change either way.
New confusion, agitation, or sudden personality changeOften a sign of an underlying medical issue, not "just aging."
Skin redness that doesn't fade over a bony areaEarly pressure injury. Far easier to prevent than to heal.
Not eating or drinking, or a sudden weight changeEscalates quickly in older adults.
Medications skipped, doubled, or confusingThe most common preventable cause of a return trip to the hospital.
Withdrawal, hopelessness, or talk of not wanting to go onTake seriously; contact their clinician. In the US, 988 reaches the Suicide & Crisis Lifeline.

Roughly one in five hospital patients is readmitted within 30 days — frequently because the transition home was never properly managed.

Frequently Asked Questions

The questions families ask us most — about the work, the cost, and their own situations.

What Gilt Is

What does a care navigator actually do?

We handle the operational layer around clinical care. That means assessing the home and the situation, fixing safety and workflow problems, sourcing and setting up equipment, translating medical instructions into a real daily routine, coordinating vendors, aligning the family on one plan, and staying available as things change.

Is this the same as a home health aide or a nurse?

No. We are not clinical providers. Nurses and aides deliver hands-on care; we build and manage the system around that care — the planning, logistics, home setup, and coordination that medical staff don't have time for and families don't know how to do.

Do you give medical advice?

Never. We translate medical instructions into practical daily systems and work alongside your medical team, never in place of them. Clinical decisions stay with your clinicians.

Do you place people in facilities?

No. Our work is centered on making home work. If a facility becomes the right path for your family, we help you think it through and stay organized — but we are not a placement agency and take no referral fees. [confirm this reflects your policy]

No. The 30-minute investigation call is completely free. Nothing is charged until after that conversation, and only if you choose to move forward. No pressure, no obligation.

What happens on that first call?

We listen. You describe what your family is navigating, we ask questions, we answer yours honestly, and together we figure out what kind of support would actually help — including telling you if that's nothing we sell.

Can you help if the situation is urgent?

Yes. Email support@giltcp.com directly and we respond the same day. In urgent situations the free call can usually be scheduled within 24 to 48 hours.

Is it too early to call? Nothing has happened yet.

Earlier is better and cheaper. The families with the hardest transitions are almost always the ones who started planning after the crisis instead of before it.

Is it too late? We've been doing this for two years already.

No. Long-running situations usually have accumulated workarounds that quietly cost the family time, money, and sleep. A fresh assessment often finds several fixable ones.

What if my parent doesn't want help?

Common, and worth taking seriously. We frame the work around preserving their independence and choices — which is usually what they're actually defending. We never make decisions about someone who is capable of making their own.
